Keyboard: Apple M0118 ("Apple Standard Keyboard"), Alps SKCM Orange switches

          the brief.

Every WAV embedded in keysound-data.js is a slice of this one 14.1-second
recording. No other keyboard, pack, or synthesized material was mixed in.

Candidates that were considered and NOT used
----------------------------------------------
- BrodieSound "Typing.wav" (CC0) -- Apple Pro Keyboard. Rejected: that
  keyboard is a scissor/membrane design, not Alps mechanical, so mixing it
  in would break the "one board, one session" rule and sound inconsistent
  next to real Alps clicks.
- zrrion "Keyboard Typing Sounds" (CC0) -- an Alps-made SM-FKB401 with SKCC
  switches, a different Alps switch family from the SKCM Orange target.
  Kept as a documented fallback only; not used, to avoid mixing switch
  families.
- cfork "computer_keyboard_hacking.aif" (CC BY 4.0) -- labeled "Apple
  extended keyboard, 2004," but only 3.3s, mono, lo-fi. Not needed once the
  robni7 recording turned out to have enough clean material by itself; kept
  as a documented CC-BY fallback (would need "by cfork" attribution if
  ever used).
- Wikimedia "Modelm.ogg" (public domain) -- IBM Model M buckling spring.
  Explicitly out of scope: Model M is a different mechanism (buckling
  spring vs. Alps click) and was to be a separate theme at most, never
  mixed into an Apple/Alps set.
- BigSoundBank s0229 "Computer Keyboard" (CC0) -- unspecified/likely modern
  board, not vintage-specific. Not used.
- OpenGameArt "Wandering Door" and Nesdood007's kde-plasma-ringtones pack:
  CC BY-SA, rejected as a share-alike license (avoidable copyleft).
- "Bucklespring" (GPL-2 sound pack): rejected, its WAVs are not usable
  under a GPL-2 source without copyleft implications for this static site.

How individual keystrokes were isolated
------------------------------------------
The source is continuous "type across the keyboard" demo audio, not
isolated single-key recordings, so there is no per-key ground truth (no
video, no keylogger reference). Processing (numpy/scipy, see scripts in
the scratchpad):
 1. Envelope + onset detection (1ms peak-hold envelope, threshold ~16dB
    above the session noise floor of -57.6dBFS) found 195 candidate
    transients across the clip.
 2. Adjacent transients were greedily paired as (press, release) when they
    were 20-115ms apart and the second was at least 3dB quieter than the
    first (consistent with Alps SKCM's louder bottom-out/press click vs.
    its quieter upstroke/release click). Unpaired loud transients were kept
    as press-only (no captured release for that instance).
 3. Each slice's window ran from ~1.5ms before the onset to either its own
    natural decay back to within 6dB of the noise floor, or the start of
    the next accepted transient minus a small guard -- whichever came
    first. Any slice that still had another accepted transient fall inside
    its window was trimmed to end just before it, or dropped if that left
    under 20ms of audio. This is why durations run shorter than the
    120/90ms (letters), 200/160ms (space), 160/120ms (Return), 140/110ms
    (Backspace/Shift) guide figures: the source is fast continuous typing,
    and not intruding on a neighboring keystroke took priority over hitting
    a target length.
 4. A short edge declick (0.4ms raised-cosine) at the very start and a
    10-25ms cosine fade-out at the tail were applied. A gentle downward
    expander (6dB-above-floor knee, ~10dB max attenuation, smoothed, never
    touching the first 15ms of attack) reduced hiss in the decay without
    gating the click itself.

Deriving Space / Return / Backspace / Shift ("derive it musically")
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Because slices are anonymous transients from a typing demo, which key
produced which sample cannot be identified directly. Per the brief's
explicit allowance to derive missing classes honestly:
 - The loudest + longest-duration press/release candidates (physically
   consistent with heavier, stabilized keys) were assigned, in order, to
   Space, then Return, then Backspace.
 - The shortest + quietest candidates were assigned to the Shift/modifier
   class (a light tap has less energy and a shorter decay).
 - Everything else (the large majority) became the Letter pool.
This is an approximation based on acoustic weight, not a verified per-key
identification, and is noted here so it isn't mistaken for one.

Loudness normalization
--------------------------
One session-wide gain was computed and applied to every slice (press and
release, all classes alike), so natural relationships are preserved:
space/Return stay more prominent than ordinary letters, and releases keep
whatever quieter level they were recorded at relative to their paired
press. The gain was chosen to put the median ordinary press near -12dBFS,
but was pulled down to -2.71dB (instead of the +2.20dB that would have hit
-12dBFS exactly) so the single loudest slice in the set (a space-bar
release peaking at -3.3dBFS raw, verified not clipped) stays at or under
-6dBFS -- the brief's explicit ceiling took priority over the median
target when the two conflicted. Median ordinary press after normalization
sits near -16.9dBFS in the file; the player applies its own additional
per-class gain on top of this at playback time (see keysound.js and the
verification report for the final numbers heard through the graph).

Format: mono 44.1kHz 16-bit PCM WAV, base64-embedded in keysound-data.js.

Sample counts (final, per class)
------------------------------------
  letter:     45 press, 28 release  (minimum required: 8 press / 5 release)
  space:       5 press,  4 release  (minimum required: 3 / 3)
  return:      5 press,  4 release  (minimum required: 3 / 3)
  backspace:   5 press,  4 release  (minimum required: 3 / 3)
  modifier:    6 press,  4 release  (minimum required: 3 / 2)

All samples are genuine distinct recorded hits -- none were duplicated or
pitch-shifted to pad these counts ("more real variants beat pitch-shifted
copies").
